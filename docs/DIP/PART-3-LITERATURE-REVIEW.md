# 3. Literature Review

## 3.1 Selection method

The literature search covered the six technical domains that govern EGIN's design decisions: barriers to the adoption of digital agriculture by smallholders, the regional context in Kazakhstan, IoT sensors and digital twins in crop farming, local-first offline synchronisation and PWAs, WebAuthn passkey usability and security, and open weather data sources.

**Selection criteria:** peer-reviewed publications from 2021–2026, supplemented by two foundational computer-science papers on local-first software and Conflict-free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs), one international organisation report (FAO) on Kazakhstan, and the official reference for the weather API used in the system. In total, the review analyses **13 sources**, including **11 scientific publications** (exceeding the minimum of 8) and **11 sources published within the last five years**.

## 3.2 Critical review

### Adoption barriers and the Kazakh context

The systematic review by Gumbi et al. demonstrates that digital agriculture systems remain much less accessible to smallholder farms than to large agro-enterprises: the primary barriers are rural connectivity infrastructure, subscription costs and digital literacy, compounded by a weak perceived value of complex platforms [1]. For Kazakhstan, Abdrazakova et al. reach consistent conclusions, highlighting weak and unstable mobile coverage in rural districts, a shortage of local IT/agronomy specialists, and the absence of a unified digital ecosystem [2]. The FAO country report likewise records low awareness of digital tools among small and medium Kazakh farmers and a weakly developed extension and advisory system [3].

A limitation of these studies is that they analyse the agricultural sector at a macro level rather than evaluating specific mobile interfaces. For EGIN, their findings translate into three strict architectural constraints: **offline-first operation**, a **low entry barrier** (phone-first UI, free web access without mandatory registration for viewing the home screen and weather), and **reuse of existing structured data** (state cadastre and 1C accounting) instead of forcing manual entry from scratch.

### Sensors and digital twins

Ndjuluwa et al. systematise IoT sensors, communication channels and network protocols in crop farming, covering soil moisture, temperature monitoring and yield estimation [4]. Recent reviews of agricultural digital twins classify crop, weather and soil models while emphasising major practical barriers: the requirement for continuous high-quality sensor streams, privacy concerns, complex multi-source integration [5], high data-management overhead [6], and the early maturity stage of crop-monitoring twins [7].

The critical engineering conclusion for EGIN is that **a 3D visualisation must not be presented as a digital twin unless backed by verified field measurements**. Accordingly, EGIN explicitly labels its 3D crop scene as an illustrative example until analytics is connected, and the sensor registry records device metadata without imitating live telemetry.

### Offline-first, synchronisation and PWA

Kleppmann et al. formulate the *local-first* software principles, placing the primary copy of user data in local storage on the client device and treating the server as a secondary synchronisation peer [8]. While Conflict-free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs) guarantee strong eventual consistency without central coordination [9], Kleppmann et al. note unresolved user-interface challenges when exposing automatic merges to end users [8], and CRDT metadata increases storage and implementation complexity.

Because plot records in a small farm are edited by one or a few people rather than concurrently by dozens of collaborators, EGIN adopts a simpler, auditable model: a monotonic server sequence cursor (`seq`), per-record integer versions, tombstones for deletions, and an **explicit user choice when a version conflict occurs**. The viability of delivering offline-capable tools to smallholder farmers via a Progressive Web App (PWA) is further supported by recent work targeting low-connectivity rural environments [12].

### Passkey authentication

Lassak et al. investigate FIDO2/WebAuthn deployment barriers and identify account recovery, cross-device friction, platform inconsistencies and user mental models as the dominant challenges [10]. In a large-scale measurement study of 872 passkey-enabled websites, Jannett et al. uncover widespread inconsistencies in key lifecycle management, continued support for deprecated algorithms, and 15 concrete attack vectors—including unauthorized passkey deletion and account lockout [11].

These two security studies directly define EGIN's authentication requirements and test cases: issuing a **one-time recovery code with automatic rotation** on use, **forbidding deletion of the last remaining passkey**, binding cross-device QR sign-in to the initiating browser cookie and a verified passkey assertion, and testing against key-deletion lockout scenarios.

### Weather data

Open-Meteo aggregates numerical weather prediction models from national meteorological services behind an open API licensed under CC BY 4.0 (attribution required, free for non-commercial use) [13]. Its limitation is that gridded forecast models do not replace in-field microclimate stations; therefore, EGIN presents Open-Meteo data strictly as a coordinate-based forecast and marks cached forecasts as stale when viewed offline.

## 3.3 Review conclusions

1. The requirement for offline-first operation and minimal onboarding friction is directly supported by both global smallholder reviews [1] and Kazakhstan-specific studies [2], [3].
2. Literature on agricultural digital twins [5]–[7] justifies strict interface honesty: separating the 3D visual scene and sensor registry from unimplemented telemetry and growth-phase computation.
3. Foundational local-first and CRDT works [8], [9] and recent USENIX Security studies on passkeys [10], [11] provide concrete architectural trade-offs and security verification scenarios for EGIN's sync engine and WebAuthn implementation.
